How we run the day

Guilford, J. P. (1956). The structure of intellect. Psychological Bulletin, 53(4), 267–293.

  • How we’re going to run today

Where “divergent” and “convergent” thinking come from: two of the operations in Guilford’s model of intelligence.

Caveat The full model has few supporters now; the divergent/convergent split survives in creativity research.

Publisher (paywalled)

Osborn, A. F. (1953). Applied Imagination: Principles and Procedures of Creative Thinking. Scribner.

  • How we’re going to run today

The book that launched brainstorming. Its two rules: defer judgement, and go for quantity.

Borrow free (archive.org)

Diehl, M. & Stroebe, W. (1987). Productivity loss in brainstorming groups: Toward the solution of a riddle. JPSP, 53(3), 497–509.

  • How we’re going to run today (notes)

Groups brainstorming out loud produce fewer ideas than the same people working alone. The main cause is waiting your turn to speak. Hence: think alone first, then share.

Publisher (paywalled)

Design Council (2005). The Double Diamond.

  • How we’re going to run today

Diverge, converge, twice: once on the problem, once on the solution.

Caveat The Design Council’s own history dates the first public version to 2004.

Language & conversation

Clark, H. H. & Brennan, S. E. (1991). Grounding in communication. In L. B. Resnick, J. M. Levine & S. D. Teasley (eds), Perspectives on Socially Shared Cognition (pp. 127–149). APA.

  • They don’t know what you know

Every conversation builds on common ground, what both people know and know the other knows, and adds to it as you go.

Free PDF (Clark’s site)

Lewis, D. (1979). Scorekeeping in a language game. Journal of Philosophical Logic, 8(1), 339–359.

  • They don’t know what you know

If you say something that assumes a fact, listeners usually accept it as background: why “as you know…” works, within limits.

Publisher (paywalled)

Camerer, C., Loewenstein, G. & Weber, M. (1989). The curse of knowledge in economic settings: An experimental analysis. Journal of Political Economy, 97(5), 1232–1254.

  • They don’t know what you know

Better-informed people can’t ignore what they know, even when it would pay them to.

Publisher (paywalled)

Newton, E. L. (1990). The rocky road from actions to intentions. PhD dissertation, Stanford University.

  • They don’t know what you know

The tapping study. Tappers drummed well-known songs and predicted listeners would name half of them; listeners got 3 out of 120.

Caveat One unpublished study, never formally replicated. Made famous by Heath & Heath’s Made to Stick.

Free PDF (copy on gwern.net)

Kauffman, S. A. (2000). Investigations. Oxford University Press.

  • They don’t know what you know

The “adjacent possible”: the next steps you can reach from where you are now. A metaphor from biology, borrowed here for where to pitch an idea.

Caveat A complexity-science idea, not a communication finding. Steven Johnson’s Where Good Ideas Come From (2010) is the friendlier read.

Borrow free (archive.org)

Aristotle. Rhetoric, book I, ch. 3 (trans. W. Rhys Roberts).

  • Talking is translating

Of speaker, subject and listener, it’s the listener who decides what a speech is for.

Free text (MIT Classics)

Kinneavy, J. L. (1971). A Theory of Discourse: The Aims of Discourse. Prentice-Hall.

  • Talking is translating

The communication triangle: writer, reader, reality, and the text between them. Our triangle relabels its corners for engineers.

Borrow free (archive.org)

Meyer, E. (2014). The Culture Map: Breaking Through the Invisible Boundaries of Global Business. PublicAffairs. Ch. 4.

  • Talking is translating (notes)

Some cultures want the conclusion first, others the reasoning first. Meyer puts Germany towards reasoning first: worth knowing before you “lead with the summary”.

amazon.de

Heath, C. & Heath, D. (2007). Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die. Random House.

  • Coin a phrase, then repeat it

What makes an idea memorable: simple, unexpected, concrete, credible, emotional, a story.

amazon.de

Hasher, L., Goldstein, D. & Toppino, T. (1977). Frequency and the conference of referential validity. Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 16(1), 107–112.

  • Coin a phrase, then repeat it

The illusory truth effect: statements we’ve heard before feel more true. So repeat your phrase, and use it for true things only.

Publisher (paywalled)

Banse, R. & Scherer, K. R. (1996). Acoustic profiles in vocal emotion expression. JPSP, 70(3), 614–636.

  • Say it three ways; they guess

Listeners recognised 14 emotions from actors’ voices alone, well above chance. Tone carries meaning the words don’t.

Free (University of Geneva)

Goldin-Meadow, S. & Alibali, M. W. (2013). Gesture’s role in speaking, learning, and creating language. Annual Review of Psychology, 64, 257–283.

  • Free your hands to think

Gesturing helps the speaker think and find words, not only the listener understand.

Free (PubMed Central)

Hohpe, G. (2020). The Software Architect Elevator: Redefining the Architect’s Role in the Digital Enterprise. O’Reilly. Ch. 18 “Explaining Stuff” and ch. 19 “Show the Kids the Pirate Ship!”.

  • Build a ramp, not a cliff
  • Show the pirate ship first

Ch. 18: don’t drop people off a cliff of jargon or leave gaps you can’t see; build the vocabulary first, and test by asking them to teach it back. Ch. 19: show what the thing is for before its parts.

amazon.de

Pickering, M. J. & Garrod, S. (2004). Toward a mechanistic psychology of dialogue. Behavioral and Brain Sciences, 27(2), 169–190.

  • Use their wording, not yours

People in conversation drift towards the same words and sentence shapes, and understand each other more easily when they do.

Free to read (publisher)

Huang, K., Yeomans, M., Brooks, A. W., Minson, J. & Gino, F. (2017). It doesn’t hurt to ask: Question-asking increases liking. JPSP, 113(3), 430–452.

  • Let them talk first
  • Prove you’re listening
  • Four real things do the work

People who ask more questions, especially follow-up questions, are liked more by the people they talk to.

Caveat Observers watching the conversation didn’t show the effect: it’s about the person being asked.

Free PDF (Harvard)Publisher

Johnstone, K. (1979). Impro: Improvisation and the Theatre. Faber & Faber.

  • Let them talk first

The improviser’s classic on status and on accepting offers instead of blocking them, which is where “yes, and” thinking comes from.

amazon.de

Walker, C. (2014). From Contempt to Curiosity: Creating the Conditions for Groups to Collaborate Using Clean Language and Systemic Modelling. Clean Publishing.

  • Swap contempt for curiosity

Using clean questions to turn a group’s judgements of each other into curiosity.

Caveat A practitioner book; there are no controlled trials of Clean Language yet.

amazon.de

Lawley, J. & Tompkins, P. (2000). Metaphors in Mind: Transformation through Symbolic Modelling. The Developing Company Press.

  • Swap contempt for curiosity

David Grove’s clean questions, such as “And what kind of X is that X?”, written up as a method.

Empathy & active listening

Voss, C. & Raz, T. (2016). Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It. Harper Business.

  • Prove you’re listening (chs 2–3)
  • Label the feeling, not the person (ch. 3)
  • Get to “that’s right” (ch. 5)
  • Ask what and how, not why (ch. 7)

A former FBI hostage negotiator on mirroring, labelling feelings, getting to “that’s right” and open “what” and “how” questions.

Caveat Experience and stories rather than studies. “Avoid why” comes from his company’s staff articles more than the book.

amazon.de

Black Swan Group (2018). Never Split the Difference: Negotiating Contracts (e-book).

  • Label the feeling, not the person

Voss’s company’s short guide to labels, mirrors, summaries and the accusation audit.

Caveat Marketing material; no evidence cited.

Black Swan Group

Bavelas, J. B., Coates, L. & Johnson, T. (2000). Listeners as co-narrators. JPSP, 79(6), 941–952.

  • Prove you’re listening
  • A fifth move is to wait

When listeners were distracted and stopped reacting, speakers told their stories worse, especially the ending. Reactions tied to the story mattered most, not just “mm-hmm”.

Publisher (paywalled)

Rowe, M. B. (1974). Wait-time and rewards as instructional variables, their influence on language, logic, and fate control: Part one, wait-time. Journal of Research in Science Teaching, 11(2), 81–94.

  • A fifth move is to wait

Teachers wait about a second for an answer. At three seconds or more, pupils gave longer answers and more of them answered.

Caveat Classrooms, not meetings.

Publisher (paywalled)Free summary (Vanderbilt)

Stivers, T. et al. (2009). Universals and cultural variation in turn-taking in conversation. PNAS, 106(26), 10587–10592.

  • A fifth move is to wait

Across ten languages, the usual gap before an answer ran from about 7 ms (Japanese) to about 470 ms (Danish). Cultures differ, but far less than the stereotypes say.

Free (PubMed Central)

Rogers, C. R. & Farson, R. E. (1957). Active Listening. Industrial Relations Center, University of Chicago.

  • Get to “that’s right”

The pamphlet for managers that named active listening. Its centre: check your understanding by saying it back.

Caveat Often dated 1987, from a later reprint.

amazon.de

Fisher, R. & Ury, W. (1981). Getting to Yes: Negotiating Agreement Without Giving In. Houghton Mifflin. Chs 3 and 4.

  • Find the need behind the position

Interests, not positions. The two sisters and the orange: one wanted the fruit, the other the peel for a cake.

Politics & power structures

Krackhardt, D. & Hanson, J. R. (1993). Informal networks: The company behind the chart. Harvard Business Review, 71(4), 104–111.

  • Draw the chart they don’t print

Map who asks whom for advice and who trusts whom; it rarely matches the org chart.

Kerr, S. (1975). On the folly of rewarding A, while hoping for B. Academy of Management Journal, 18(4), 769–783.

  • Find out what they’re measured on

People do what they’re rewarded for, not what you hope they’ll do.

Publisher (paywalled)

Appelo, J. Moving Motivators. Management 3.0.

  • Ask where they want to go next
  • Follow the five threads

A card game with ten motivators for talking about what drives someone.

Management 3.0

Liker, J. K. (2004). The Toyota Way: 14 Management Principles from the World’s Greatest Manufacturer. McGraw-Hill. Principle 13.

  • Pre-wire the decision

Nemawashi: decide slowly by consensus, talking to everyone before the meeting; then act fast.

König, O. & Schattenhofer, K. (2006; 10th edn 2020). Einführung in die Gruppendynamik. Carl-Auer.

  • The task is only the tip
  • Every group has two edges

A short German introduction to group dynamics: what happens under the task, and the pulls of belonging, power and closeness.

Caveat Ways of looking at a group, not findings to test. No English translation.

  • Rank is a position

Rank dynamics: alpha, beta, gamma and omega are positions in a group, not personalities.

Caveat Some sources date it 1956. Reprinted in Schindler’s selected writings, Das lebendige Gefüge der Gruppe (Psychosozial-Verlag, 2016).

The circle of concern and the circle of influence: put your energy where you can change something.

Mostly nonsense: what didn’t survive testing

Wood, J. V., Perunovic, W. Q. E. & Lee, J. W. (2009). Positive self-statements: Power for some, peril for others. Psychological Science, 20(7), 860–866.

  • Mostly nonsense

Repeating “I’m a lovable person” made people with low self-esteem feel worse.

Caveat Small samples.

Publisher (paywalled)

Wiseman, R. et al. (2012). The eyes don’t have it: Lie detection and Neuro-Linguistic Programming. PLoS ONE, 7(7), e40259.

  • Mostly nonsense

NLP says eye movements give liars away. Three studies found they don’t, and training people in the cues didn’t help.

Free (PLoS ONE)

Brown, N. J. L., Sokal, A. D. & Friedman, H. L. (2013). The complex dynamics of wishful thinking: The critical positivity ratio. American Psychologist, 68(9), 801–813.

  • Mostly nonsense

Took apart the maths behind the famous 3:1 “positivity ratio”; the ratio was formally withdrawn.

Free preprint (arXiv)Publisher

With 200 people, power poses changed how powerful people felt, but not their hormones or risk-taking.
